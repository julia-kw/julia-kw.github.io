    Section 2.5 The standard deviation

    The past two classes we’ve been looking at different ways of understanding the spread of a data set. It’d be nice if there were a single number we could use to summarize how spread out a data set is, akin to how there is a single number (the mean) we can use to summarize the center of a data set. Of course, we shouldn’t expect this single number to tell us everything about the spread, much like the mean isn’t the full story. But it’d be nice to have regardless.
    🔗
    Good news. We can do this. That’s what this section is about.
    🔗
    Here’s an idea of how you might go about that. First identify the center of the data set. Next, determine the average distance of a datum from the center. This will give you a single number which represents how spread out the data are. A small value says that the average distance to the center is small while a large value says the average distance to the center is large.
    🔗
    At core, this is a good idea for a measure of spread. However, for technical reasons we want to slightly tweak it to get something that is better behaved mathematically. Instead of looking at the average distance to the center we get the average of the squares of the distances. Then to get back to the correct units we take the square root.
    🔗

    Definition 2.5.1. Standard deviation.

    Consider a data set
    \begin{equation*} x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n \end{equation*}
    of \(n\) many data with mean \(\bar x\text{.}\) The variance \(\mathrm{Var}\) of the data set is the average of the squares of the distances from the mean, and the standard deviation \(s\) is the square root of that. As formulas:
    \begin{align*} \mathrm{Var} \amp= \frac{(x_1 - \bar x)^2 + (x_2 - \bar x)^2 + \cdots + (x_n - \bar x)^2}{n} = \frac{ \sum (x_i - \bar x)^2}{n},\\ s \amp= \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}} = \sqrt{\frac{ \sum (x_i - \bar x)^2}{n}}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    The more important of these two notions is the standard deviation. You can think of the variance as a step to computing the standard deviation—first compute the variance, that is the average of the squares of the distances from the mean, and then take its square root to get the standard deviation. As said earlier, the reason to take squares of distances instead of distances is that it has nicer mathematical properties which eases more advanced uses. Taking the square root at the end returns us to the original unit. If, for example, our data are weights of fish in pounds, then the mean is also in pounds. Because we square, the units for the variance is square pounds. Taking a square root gets us back to pounds, the same units we started with.
    🔗

    Example 2.5.2.

    Consider the three data sets from earlier:
    \begin{equation*} -2,0,2 \qquad\qquad -10,0,10 \qquad\qquad -2000,0,2000. \end{equation*}
    Let’s compute their standard deviations in turn. We do the first in detail. Before you can do anything else you need to know the mean, in this case we already saw that \(\bar x = 0\text{.}\) Now we need to take the average of the squares of the distances from the mean:
    \begin{equation*} 2^2 + 0^2 + 2^2 = 4 + 0 + 4 = 8. \end{equation*}
    We then divide that by the number of data and take the square root, to get the standard deviation is
    \begin{equation*} s = \sqrt{\frac 83} \approx 1.63. \end{equation*}
    For the other two data sets we do the same process. The second data set has standard deviation \(\approx 8.16\) and the third data set has standard devation \(\approx 1633\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Warning! Due to technical considerations (the keyword to search is “Bessel’s correction”) most statistical software packages will use a slightly different formula for their standard deviation function. Namely, they will divide by \(n-1\) instead of \(n\text{.}\) This is the so-called corrected standard deviation or unbiased estimator of the standard deviation, as upposed to the uncorrected standard deviation I gave. This is something to watch out for if you use computer tools to check your work. They might give a slightly different answer because they used a slightly different formula.
    🔗
    I promised you that the standard deviation has some nice mathematical properties. Let me mention one. Consider the following two data sets:
    \begin{equation*} -8, 8 \qquad\text{and}\qquad -80,80. \end{equation*}
    Note that the second data set came from the first one by multiplying everything by \(10\text{.}\) Both data sets have the same mean, namely \(0\text{.}\) If you compute their standard deviations you get \(8\) and \(80\text{.}\) That is, multiplying everything by \(10\) gave you a standard deviation \(10\) times as large.
    🔗
    In general, if a data set has standard deviation \(s\text{,}\) then if you multiply every datum by \(k\) the new data set has standard deviation \(ks\text{.}\) This is similar to a property enjoyed by the mean—multiplying all values by \(k\) multiplies the mean by \(k\text{.}\)
    🔗
