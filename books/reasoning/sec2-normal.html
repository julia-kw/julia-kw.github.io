    Section 2.6 Normal distributions

    In the previous sections we looked at various methods of summarizing data. An important use of statistics is to go beyond summaries and make inferences about not-yet known data. This is a huge topic, and we will not explore it fully. Nonetheless, in this section we move toward that use.
    🔗
    Step zero of doing statistics is to get something you can quantify. There is an unknown distribution of the quantities across a population. Doing an experiment you get a data set which is a sample from the population. You then want to use that sample to make inferences about the distribution.
    🔗

    Example 2.6.1.

    Perhaps you are a entomologist studying centipedes. As part of larger work you want to know the size of a species. The distribution is the different lengths across members of the species. As a sample you look at some centipedes and measure their lengths.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.6.2.

    You are a pharmaceutical researcher studying a potential new blood pressure medication. The distribution you want to know about is blood pressures of people who take the medication versus those who don’t. Your samples come from a drug trial where you measure what happens with a group given the drug versus a control group.
    🔗
    🔗
    Given a distribution a statistic is some number that can be calculated from it. Typical examples are the mean or standard deviation. Because the overall distribution is unknown, you don’t know the value. You want to use your sample to calculate an estimator, something you can calculate from your sample data which estimates the true value of the statistic. For example, you might estimate the population mean by calculating the sample mean.
    🔗

    Example 2.6.3.

    You don’t know the true average length of your centipede species. To estimate this unknown value, you compute the mean length of centipedes in your sample. If your sampling method is unbiased and your sample is large enough, this estimator will give a good estimate of the true mean.
    🔗
    On the other hand, if your sampling method is biased it will produce a poor estimate. For example, if your method of catching centipedes only grabs those above a certain length, you will overestimate the true mean length.
    🔗
    🔗
    There are many different shapes a distribution can take. Often real-world applications are best modeled as continuous distributions, those which can take on a continuous range of values. We represent these with a graph showing the likelihood of different values. If you randomly sample to measure a value, the likelihood it sits between a minimum \(a\) and a maximum \(b\) is the area under the graph between \(a\) and \(b\text{.}\)
    🔗
    
    A normal distribution given by a bell-shaped curve. (See below for more about normal distributions.) A region symmetric across the mean is filled in. The area of this region represents the likelihood that a randomly chosen sample falls into the range. In this case, the range happens to be within one standard deviation of the mean.
    🔗

    Example 2.6.4.

    A uniform distribution is one where all outcomes in a range are equally likely. These are represented by graphs which are horizontal lines from the minimum to the maximum of the range. The total area must be \(1\text{,}\) because there is a \(100\%\) chance the value falls somewhere in the range.
    🔗
    
    The uniform distribution between \(0\) and \(10\text{,}\) represented by a horizontal line. A region is shaded in, to represent the likelihood a random sample is between \(3\) and \(6\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For example, a uniform distribution with values ranging from \(0\) to \(10\) would be represented by a horizontal line of height \(\frac 1{10}\text{.}\) The likelihood a randomly sampled value falls between \(3\) and \(6\) is the area of the region between \(3\) and \(6\text{,}\) namely
    \begin{equation*} (6 - 3) \cdot \frac1{10} = \frac{3}{10}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    In general, a uniform distribution with values ranging from \(A\) to \(B\) is represented by a horizontal line of height \(\frac{1}{B - A}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    
    The uniform distribution between \(A\) and \(B\text{,}\) represented by a horizontal line. A region is shaded in, to represent the likelihood a random sample falls into a certain subrange.
    🔗
    🔗
    Often in practice you can know that a population fits (approximately) a distribution of a certain shape, even if you don’t know the specifics. When this happens it is quite helpful in making predictions about the population, as it narrows down the possibilities. The most common case of this is with the normal distribution.
    🔗

    Definition 2.6.5. Normal distributions.

    A normal distribution is a kind of distribution which is shaped like a bell curve. If you randomly sample, most values will be near the mean, with farther away outliers being more and more unlikely. A normal distribution is specified by two parameters, its mean and standard deviation.
    🔗
    
    A normal distribution. A bell-shaped curve with a mean at \(x = 0\text{.}\) As you go out from the mean the curve gets closer and closer to the axis. The height of the curve above the horizontal axis represents the likelihood of that value. That is, values close to the mean are more likely and outliers are less likely.
    🔗
    🔗
    Normal distributions are important because we know many things in practice are normal, or approximately normal. Consider the example of repeatedly rolling a (six-sided) die and averaging the results. If you roll it once, any of the six outcomes are equally likely. But if you roll it many times then you expect to see high rolls counterbalance low rolls. It’s not likely to roll a long string of \(6\)s. As such, if you roll the die a lot and take the mean of the results, it’s most likely to be close to the true mean of the results, namely
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6}{6} = 3.5. \end{equation*}
    Outliers are possible, but the farther you get from \(3.5\) the less likely it is to see that result.
    🔗

    Theorem 2.6.6. The central limit theorem.

    Suppose you take an unbiased random sample from any distribution. Call the mean of this distribution \(\mu\) and its standard deviation \(\sigma\text{.}\) (These are the Greek letters “mu” and “sigma”, the counterparts to the Latin m and s, for mean and standard deviation.) You sample \(n\) times and calculate the mean \(\bar x\) of the sample. This sample mean \(\bar x\) is itself a random variable; different samples will produce a different mean according to chance.
    🔗
    The distribution of this random value \(\bar x\) is approximately a normal distribution, provided the number of samples \(n\) is large enough. Indeed, this normal distribution has mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    This is a bit mathy, so let’s lay it out in plain language. No matter what distribution you are sampling from, no matter how it’s laid out, if you calculate the sample mean you know what the distribution for that result looks like. It is a normal distribution with the same mean, and its spread (standard deviation) decreases as the sample size increases. That is, the larger your sample the more closely clustered you expect the outcome to be.
    🔗

    Example 2.6.7.

    Let’s look at the dice example more carefully. This is a distribution with six possible values, each equally likely. We already calculated its mean:
    \begin{equation*} \mu = \frac{1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6}{6} = 3.5. \end{equation*}
    With a little effort we can also calculate its standard deviation:
    \begin{align*} \sigma \amp= \frac{1}{6} \sum (i - 3.5)^2\\ \amp= \frac{6.25 + 2.25 + 0.25 + 0.25 + 2.25 + 6.25}{6}\\ \amp= \frac{35}{12} \approx 2.917. \end{align*}
    What this means is, if you roll a die once on average the value will be \(3.5\) but the results are spread out. It’s reasonably likely to be far from \(3.5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    On the other hand, if you repeatedly roll the die and take the average result, the central limit theorem tells us the distribution for the result is a bell curve. Moreover, we can calculate its standard deviation. For example, if you roll \(30\) times then the standard deviation is
    \begin{equation*} \sigma/\sqrt{30} \approx 0.533. \end{equation*}
    That is, most of the time your result from rolling thirty times will be very close to the mean of \(3.5\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Calculating likelihoods in general using a bell curve requires computer tools. But there’s a few common ranges you can remember.
    • \(68\%\) of values are within \(1\) standard deviation of the mean.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(95\%\) of values are within \(2\) standard deviations of the mean.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(99.7\%\) of values are within \(3\) standard deviations of the mean.
      🔗
      🔗
    Another useful fact is that bell curves are symmetric across their mean. This implies that the mean is also the median, but it has other consequences. For example, if a region is symmetric across the mean then half its area is above the mean and half is below.
    🔗
    Going back to our dice rolling example, if you roll a die \(30\) times and take the average of the results, about \(68\%\) of the time that average will be between \(3\) and \(4\text{.}\)
    🔗
