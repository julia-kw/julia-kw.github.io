    Section 2.7 Hypothesis testing

    In the previous section we talked about distributions. These describe the shape of the overall data about a population. Directly measuring distributions in the real world is impractical. For instance, if you were a scientist studying childhood nutrition, one distribution you may be interested in is the weights of children across the nation. This consists of millions of people, and it’s infeasible to measure every child.
    🔗
    Despite this limitation, we can still make inferences about populations. For example, you might be trying to confirm a hypothesis a government program to give money to parents improves childhood nutrition. You could test this by looking at a sample of children whose parents were part of the program versus those who weren’t. If your hypothesis is correct, you would expect to see a lower rate of underweight children among those in the program, and if your hypothesis is wrong you’d expect to see similar rights of underweightedness.
    🔗
    Of course, there is always some randomness involved, since you aren’t surveying the entire, too large population of all children. Complete surety is thereby impossible. What you want is a measure of how likely it is that your hypothesis is correct. Unfortunately, that is also difficult to determine. Instead you rely on statistical proxies to give you some evidence.
    🔗
    This is the basics of what statisticians call hypothesis testing. This is a large, complicated topic. So we will not go over it in detail. Instead, let’s talk through an overview. The goal is not to be able to go out and do hypothesis testing yourself. Rather, the goal is to understand it well enough that you can critically evaluate others use of it.
    🔗
    Perhaps the most used proxy used as evidence for a hypothesis is the p-value. If you have looked at a scientific or medical article ever, you may have seen little parentheticals like “\(p \lt 0.05\)” or “\(p \lt 1\%\)”. These are the p-values for the associated claim. A low p-value is taken as reason to believe a claim, with a common threshold of \(5\%\text{.}\)
    🔗
    A common misconception is that a p-value is the chance that a claim is wrong. So, for example, \(p \lt 5\%\) would mean there’s a less than one in twenty chance the claim is wrong. But that is not something we can measure, so that can’t be what a p-value means.
    🔗
    Instead, this is the meaning. You oppose your hypothesis to a null hypothesis you wish to reject. Usually, the null hypothesis is that there is no change or difference. You do your random sampling or experiment and collect your sample results, and compute an estimator based on that sample. You then calculate, based on some knowledge of the shape of the distribution, how likely it is, assuming the null hypothesis, to see results at least as extreme as what you measured. This probability is the p-value.
    🔗

    Example 2.7.1.

    You know the mean length for this species of centipede is \(14.2\) centimeters. You measured that the mean length of centipedes fed a special diet as part of an experiment is \(15.1\) centimeters. You do a hypothesis test and calculate a p-value of \(0.03\text{.}\) That is, under the assumption that the special diet has no effect, there’s only a \(3\%\) chance of getting a sample mean at least \(0.9\) centimeters above the mean. This p-value is small, so you have some confidence your intervention has effect.
    🔗
    🔗
    Performing the probability calculation in a hypothesis test requires you to know something about the shape of your distribution. This is where theoretical results in statistics come in. People mathematically proved that the data must look approximately like such-and-such, and so we can leverage some knowledge even if we don’t know the specifics. For example, the central limit theorem from the previous section tells us that if you are doing a hypothesis test about a sample mean then you know the data are (approximately) normally distributed. This enables statisticians to use tools about normal distributions to make the calculations for finding p-values. Depending on how much is known about the data, different tests are appropriate, such as z-test or a t-test.
    🔗
    Other distributions also arise in practice, with their own associated hypothesis tests. Part of the task of learning statistics is learning the different tests and when they are applicable. But they come back to this same general phenomenon. Theoretical results tell you something about the distribution for an estimator for the statistic. You do an experiment and calculate the estimator. Then you calculate how likely it is to see a result at least that extreme, under the assumption of the null hypothesis. If this probability, called a p-value, is low, that is taken as reason to reject the null hypothesis.
    🔗
    You might think this is a bit loose. Isn’t statistics supposed to be about getting precision in our knowledge? We might not know perfectly, but we want to say we’re probably very close.
    🔗
    But there’s a lot of assumptions going into all of this work, and what we’re measuring isn’t even the thing we want in the first place. There’s some arbitrariness. For example, why pick \(5\%\) as the threshold for p-values? Why not \(1\%\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Ultimately, statistics is a very human affair. The promise of avoiding all bias and setting out an Objective Truth That All Can Agree Upon is a false one. We can get information, and we can become more sure than before in our knowledge. But we must not be misled by numbers into thinking we have more than we do.
    🔗
    This then was the goal of this section: for you to understand enough about hypothesis testing to not be taken in by misconceptions as to its capabilities. This goal goes in hand with the larger goal in this chapter of critical engagement with statistics. Part of the homework for this section will ask you to practice this.
