    Section 3.4 Exponential models

    Exponential functions are useful for model phenomena where the rate of change of a quantity is proportional to its current size. In this section we look at several such applications.
    🔗
    One example of this is population models. Suppose you have a population of organisms, such as bacteria cells or humans. These organisms reproduce. The specifics—how long does it take? how many new organisms are produced?—differ by species, and there’s a simultaneous process of death happening. If there are sufficient resources to support the entire population then the rate of change of the population will be approximately proportional to its size. If you, say, doubled the population that would be twice as many organisms to reproduce and die, so the rate of change would be doubled.
    🔗
    This isn’t exact, of course. But it can make a useful model if we keep in mind its limitations.
    🔗

    Definition 3.4.1. Population growth via an exponential model.

    A population \(P(t)\) over time \(t\) can be modeled by the function
    \begin{equation*} P(t) = P_0 e^{rt}, \end{equation*}
    where \(P_0\) is the initial value of the population, at the time \(t = 0\) we start from, and and \(r\) is a constant which expresses the rate of change.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.4.2.

    In the year \(2000\text{,}\) the global population was estimated to be approximately \(6.15\) billion people. In \(2010\text{,}\) that increased to approximately \(6.99\) billion. We can use this to make an exponential model.
    \begin{equation*} P(t) = P_0 e^{rt} \text{ billion people } t \text{ years after } 2000. \end{equation*}
    We need to determine the values of the two parameters \(P_0\) and \(r\text{.}\) The first is straightforward: \(P_0 = 6.15\) is the initial value. Once we know \(P_0\) we can find \(r\) using a little algebra.
    \begin{align*} 6.99 \amp= 6.15 e^{r\cdot 10}\\ \frac{6.99}{6.15} \amp= e^{10r} \end{align*}
    To solve for \(r\) we use the device of a logarithm. Logarithms are the opposites of exponential functions, akin to how square roots are the opposite of squaring. In this case we use the natural logarithm \(\ln\text{,}\) the logarithm with base \(e\text{,}\) in order to match the base of the exponential. Any scientific calculator will have a button for \(\ln\text{.}\) If we take the natural logarithm of both sides of the equation we get an equation where we can solve for \(r\text{.}\)
    \begin{align*} 10r \amp= \ln\left(\frac{6.99}{6.15}\right)\\ r \amp= \frac1{10}\ln\left(\frac{6.99}{6.15}\right)\\ r \amp\approx 0.0128 \end{align*}
    Plugging in these values we get our exponential model.
    \begin{equation*} P(t) = 6.15 e^{0.0128t}. \end{equation*}
    We can use this to make predictions. For instance, this model estimates the population at \(2020\) to be
    \begin{equation*} P(20) = 6.15 e^{0.0128 \cdot 20} approx 7.94 \text{ billion}. \end{equation*}
    This is a little higher than the actual population in \(2020\) of \(7.89\) billion. The reason for the mismatch is that the rate of change as a percentile hasn’t been the same year by year. The \(0.0128 = 1.28\%\) we calculated is accurate for the decade of the \(2000\)s, but the rate of change has slightly declined in the years after. Thus using this value leads to an overestimate.
    🔗
    A more accurate model would take into account the change in the rate of change over time. But that comes at the cost of being more complicated to work with.
    🔗
    🔗
    Our next use of exponential models is one where a quantity is decreasing over time.
    🔗

    Definition 3.4.3. Exponential decay.

    A quantity \(Q(t)\) which is decreasing at a rate proportional to its size is said to be undergoing exponential decay and can be modeled with an exponential function.
    \begin{equation*} Q(t) = Q_0 e^{-rt}, \end{equation*}
    where \(Q_0\) is the initial value, the value at time \(t = 0\text{,}\) and \(r\) is a positive number giving the rate of change. That is, the exponent \(-r\) is negative, representing that the quantity gets smaller over time, not larger.
    🔗
    🔗
    One example of exponential decay is radioactive decay. For chemistry reasons we won’t go into, some substances are radioactive. What this means is that they emit subatomic particles, so that the atoms decay into a different element. For example, the isotope Uranium-235 decays into Thorium-231.
    🔗
    Different substances decay at different rates. The usual measure is called the half-life, how long it takes for half of the original substance to decay.
    🔗

    Example 3.4.4.

    U-235 has a half-life of \(704\) million years. We can use this to determine an exponential model for how much U-235 will remain starting from, say, one kilogram.
    \begin{equation*} U(t) = 1 \cdot e^{-rt} \text{ kilograms after } t \text{ hundred million years}. \end{equation*}
    We only have to determine the value for \(r\text{.}\) We do this similar to what we did with the population model.
    \begin{align*} 0.5 \amp= e^{-r \cdot 7.04}\\ \ln(0.5) \amp= -7.04 r\\ r \amp= -\ln(0.5)/7.04\\ r \amp\approx 0.0985 \end{align*}
    Thus we get that we can model the quantity of U-235 with
    \begin{equation*} U(t) = e^{-0.0985t}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Note that the value we got for \(r\) depended on our choice of units for time. If we had measured \(t\) in millions of years or in years we’d get a different value for \(r\text{.}\) Intuitively, I hope this makes sense. This constant \(r\) is a measure of the rate of change. Of course the rate of change will depend on your choice of units for time. The choice of a smaller unit for time will result in a smaller \(r\text{,}\) because the amount of change is smaller in a smaller unit of time. In this case, measuring \(t\) in millions of years or in years will increase the number of zeroes after the decimal point in \(r\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Next let’s look at a step more complicated model based on exponential functions. Experience tells you that if you place a hot object in a reservoir of cold water, then it will cool down. If the reservoir is made colder, the object will cool down faster. And the same works in reverse; if you place a cold object in a hot reservoir then it will heat up, with the rate increasing if the reservoir is hotter.
    🔗
    This experience is the starting point for a principle Isaac Newton discover and which now bears his name. Newton’s law of cooling states that the rate of heat transfer between an object and its environment is proportional to the difference in the temperatures. This proportion depends upon the material of the object and the environment it is in.
    🔗
    This heat transfer goes both ways; a hot object gets cooler while the cold reservoir gets warmer. If, however, the reservoir is large enough then the heat must be spread across a large volume and so the temperature change is minimal. In this case we can assume as a simplification that the reservoir’s temperature is constant and so the only thing changing is the temperature of the object.
    🔗
    Since the rate of change of the object’s temperature is proportional to a quantity—namely, its difference with the ambient temperature—you might expect that to model this we use an exponential function. You might be correct.
    🔗

    Definition 3.4.5. Newton’s law of cooling.

    Suppose an object with temperature \(T(t)\) in terms of time \(t\) is placed in a reservoir with ambient temperature \(T_\mathrm{res}\text{.}\) We assume that \(T_\mathrm{res}\) is constant. Then we can model the temperature as follows.
    \begin{equation*} T(t) = T_\mathrm{res} + (T_0 - T_\mathrm{res}) e^{-rt}, \end{equation*}
    where \(T_0\) is the initial value \(T(0)\) and \(r\) is a positive constant which determines the rate of change. That is, the exponent \(-rt\) is negative, representing that the difference in temperatures decreases down to zero as time goes forward.
    🔗
    🔗
    It’s known that Newton’s law of cooling is only accurate in specific circumstances. This is a common phenomenon with applications of mathematics. There are other models that are more applicable, but the physics that goes into them is more involved. For our purposes we will only look at this one, relatively simple model.
    🔗

    Example 3.4.6.

    You have a pot of water at \(100^\circ\) Celsius, just on the verge of boiling. You place an egg from the refrigerator into the water. When you put the egg in, its temperature is \(4^\circ\) C. After one minute its temperature has increased to \(26^\circ\) C. How long until the egg reaches a temperature of at least \(90^\circ\text{?}\)
    🔗
    We can use Newton’s law of cooling to model this and thereby calculate a prediction. We are given \(T_\mathrm{res} = 100\) and \(T_0 = 4\text{.}\) Plugging these in, we get
    \begin{equation*} T(t) = 100 - 96 e^{-rt} \text{ degrees C after } t \text{ minutes}. \end{equation*}
    The way you can think of this is, the \(e^{-rt}\) term approaches \(0\) as \(t\) increases to infinity. So the amount we are subtracting decreases over time, so that the temperature of the egg approaches \(100\) degrees C.
    🔗
    The remaining parameter, \(r\text{,}\) needs to be calculated. We can do this by using the measurement of the temperature at one minute.
    \begin{align*} 26 \amp= 100 - 96e^{-r \cdot 1} \\ 96e^{-r} \amp= 74 \\ e^{-r} \amp= \frac{74/96} \\ -r \amp= \ln(74/96) \\ r \amp= -\ln(74/96) \\ r \amp\approx 0.26 \end{align*}
    Our model is thus \(T(t) = 100 - 96 e^{-0.26t}\text{.}\) We can determine when the egg reaches \(90\) degrees by plugging that in for \(T(t)\) and solving for \(t\text{.}\) This is much like how we just solved for \(r\text{,}\) so I omit the steps.
    \begin{equation*} t \approx 8.69 \end{equation*}
    That is, it takes a little less than eight and three quarters minutes for the egg to reach \(90\) degrees.
    🔗
    🔗
    Graphing \(T(t)\) is instructive. It shows you that as \(t\) increases the temperature \(T(t)\) asymptotically approaches \(100\text{,}\) the ambient temperature. If instead you modeled a case of an object starting at a higher temperature than the reservoir, you would get the opposite behavior. You would get a graph that asymptotically decreases toward \(T_\mathrm{res}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Exercises Exercises

    1.

    A bacteria culture in a petri dish starts with \(100\) cells. After five hours it has grown to \(160\) cells. Create an exponential model for the number of cells in the culture. Use your model to predict how many cells there will be after \(24\) hours. Also predict how long it will be until there are \(10,000\) cells.
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    Lily pads overtake a pond one spring. On March \(15\) the lily pads cover two square meters of the pond. On March \(30\) they cover six square meters. Create an exponential growth model which models the size of the lily pad coverage. Use your model to predict how much of the pond they will cover by April \(30\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    Does it make sense to have an exponential model for a population which is decreasing over time? Explain. As part of your explanation, say which aspect of the formula for the model you would look at to identify a population decrease versus increase.
    🔗
    🔗

    4.

    The isotope Carbon-15 (C-15) has a half-life of \(2.5\) seconds. Create an exponential growth model for a quantity of C-15 starting with \(1000\) kilograms. How long will it take for there to be less than one gram of C-15 remaining?
    🔗
    Hint.
    In case the SI prefixes are unclear, a gram is \(1/1000 = 0.001\) kilograms.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5.

    A metal rod is heated to \(670^\circ\) C. It is placed inside a water bath at a temperature of \(20^\circ\) C. After one minute the rod’s temperature has decreased to \(420^\circ\) C. Use Newton’s law of cooling to model the temperature of the rod. Use your model to predict how long it will take for the rod to get below \(30^\circ\) C.
    🔗
    🔗

    6.

    Use the global population model from earlier in this section. Based on this model, what does it predict the population to be in \(2100\text{?}\) In \(2200\text{?}\) In \(2300\text{?}\) In \(2500\text{?}\) In \(3000\text{?}\) Do you think these are realistic predictions. Either explain why, or else explain why the assumptions going into the model are limited and write a sentence or two about what would be needed for a more accurate model?
