    Section 12.2 Counting and probability

    The previous section was heavy with definitions. Now that we’ve established what we’re talking about with probability the next task is how do we actually calculate things.
    🔗
    More advanced applications of probability use sample spaces with infinitely many possible outcomes. For example, if you want to model getting the weight of a random fish from Cape Cod then it makes sense to use (positive) real numbers as the possible weights. There are infinitely many real numbers, even if you restrict to a range of numbers, so the sample space will have infinitely many atomic outcomes in it.
    🔗
    These applications are more difficult and require ideas from calculus to fully handle. As such we will set them aside and only look at finite sample spaces. That is, we will only consider scenarios where there are finitely many atomic outcomes.
    🔗

    Aside
    The calculus idea used here is one that we touched against when talking about geometry, namely the integral. A beautiful thing about mathematics is how disparate fields—in this case, geometry and probability—can make use of the same concepts.
    🔗
    Calculations are easiest in the scenario when all atomic outcomes are equally likely, and by happy coincidence this is a common scenario.
    🔗

    Definition 12.2.1. Uniform probability.

    A probability space is said to be uniform if all atomic outcomes have the same probability.
    🔗
    For a sample space \(S\text{,}\) we use the set-theoretic notation \(\card S\) to denote the number of atomic outcomes in \(S\text{.}\) Thus, the probability of an atomic outcome will be
    \begin{equation*} \frac1{\card S}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    The reason the probability of each atomic outcome must be \(\frac 1{\card S}\) is that the probability of the total space is always \(1\text{.}\) Since that total probability mass is divided equally between \(\card S\) pieces we can get their individual probabilities.
    🔗
    When talking about uniform probability, it’s common for people to say something like “a card is picked at random from a deck”, with the phrase “at random” indicating that the selection process is uniform. This is perhaps a misnomer—after all, a weighted dice produces a random outcome but some results are more common than others—which is why in stuffier contexts you will see it more clearly said as “uniformly at random”.
    🔗

    Theorem 12.2.2. Fundamental property of uniform probability.

    Consider an event \(E\) in a finite, uniform probability space. Let \(S\) denote the sample space. Then,
    \begin{equation*} P(E) = \frac{\card E}{\card S}. \end{equation*}
    That is, calculating probabilities in a uniform probability space amounts to doing two counting problems: count the number of outcomes in the event and count the total number of possible outcomes.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Use the additivity property of probability. The event \(E\) consists of \(\card E\) many atomic outcomes. Because they are atomic, these outcomes don’t overlap at all. Thus \(P(E)\) must be what you get when you add \(\card E\) many copies of \(\frac 1{\card S}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    In a slogan, uniform probability is about counting.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 12.2.3.

    You roll a \(\drm 6\text{.}\) How likely are you to roll a prime number? What if you roll a \(\drm 8\text{?}\) A \(\drm 12\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The prime numbers up to \(12\) are \(2, 3, 5, 7, 11\text{.}\) To calculate these probabilities we need to count the number of primes and the number of sides on the die. The second count is easy, as it’s given to us.
    \begin{align*} \drm 6:\qquad \amp P(\mathrm{prime}) = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2}\\ \drm 8:\qquad \amp P(\mathrm{prime}) = \frac 48 = \frac 12\\ \drm 12:\qquad \amp P(\mathrm{prime}) = \frac{5}{12} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 12.2.4.

    You draw a card at random from a deck of playing cards. How likely is it that you draw a face card?
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 12.2.5.

    You roll \(2\drm 6\text{.}\) How likely is it that they sum to \(7\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The easiest way to think about this is to consider as your sample space all possible rolls of the two dice. Thus there are \(6^2 = 36\) different atomic outcomes. That gives the denominator for the probability. It remains to get the count for the numerator. To do this, we can list out all outcomes which sum to \(7\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} 1 + 6, 2 + 5, 3 + 4, 4 + 3, 5 + 2, 6 + 1. \end{equation*}
    Thus the probability of rolling a \(7\) is
    \begin{equation*} \frac 6{36} = \frac 16. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 12.2.6.

    You are playing poker with a single deck. You and your two friends have the following hands.
    \begin{align*} \mathrm{You}: \qquad \amp 9\diamondsuit\ 9\clubsuit\ 9\heartsuit\ 4\diamondsuit\ 2\heartsuit\\ \mathrm{Alice}: \qquad \amp A\heartsuit\ 8\diamondsuit\ 7\diamondsuit\ 4\heartsuit\ 3\diamondsuit \\ \mathrm{Bob}: \qquad \amp K\heartsuit\ Q\spadesuit\ J\clubsuit\ 5\spadesuit 2\diamondsuit \end{align*}
    You are the first to get a chance to discard and redraw to complete your five card hand. You decide to discard your \(2\) to attempt to get a second \(4\) to complete a full house of three \(9\)s and two \(4\)s. How likely are you to succeed? How likely are you to either get your full house or instead draw the final \(9\) for a four of a kind?
    🔗
    Hint.
    While the full deck has \(52\) cards in it, you can’t draw a card that’s already on the table.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    In theory, you can always calculate a (uniform) probability by counting up the number of outcomes in the event and dividing by the total number of possible outcomes. In practice, however, this is not always feasible. We want to have some shortcuts that allow us simplify counting problems. We can use facts about probability to enable these shortcuts.
    🔗

    Definition 12.2.7. Complementary events.

    The complement of an event \(E\) is the event \(F =\) “\(E\) doesn’t happen”. In set theoretic language, this is saying that \(F = S \setminus E\text{,}\) where \(S\) is the sample space. We write \(\bar E\) for the complement of \(E\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    For example, in the probability space of rolling a \(\drm 6\text{,}\) the complement of the event “rolling an even number” is “rolling an odd number”. Note that the complement of the complement of \(E\) is always \(E\) itself.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 12.2.8.

    What is the complement of the empty event \(\emptyset\text{?}\) What is the complement of the sample space \(S\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 12.2.9. Probability of complements.

    If \(E\) is an event then
    \begin{equation*} P(\bar E) = 1 - P(E). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Use the additivity property of probability. The events \(E\) and \(\bar E\) are disjoint and their union is the entire sample space \(S\text{.}\) Thus
    \begin{equation*} P(E) + P(\bar E) = P(S) = 1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 12.2.10.

    You roll \(2\drm6\text{.}\) How likely is it that you don’t roll a \(7\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Earlier we calculated that the probability of rolling a \(7\) was \(\frac 16\text{.}\) Thus the probability of not rolling a \(7\) is
    \begin{equation*} 1 - \frac16 = \frac 56. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 12.2.11. Independence.

    Two events \(E\) and \(F\) are independent if, intuitively, whether one happens doesn’t affect the likelihood of the other. Formally, this idea is captured by the equation
    \begin{equation*} P(E \cap F) = P(E) \cdot P(F). \end{equation*}
    That is, two events are independent if the probability they both happen is the product of their individual probabilities.
    🔗
    🔗
    Most often, the utility of independence is that it lets you use that equation. If it’s clear from the definition of events that they are independent then that equation lets you replace a more difficult count with two easier counts.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 12.2.12.

    You roll two \(\drm20\)s. How likely is it that both results are at least \(10\text{?}\) How likely is it that at least one result is at least \(10\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    You could solve these by listing out all \(20^2 = 400\) possible pairs of results and counting from them. But \(400\) is a big number and that sounds like a lot of work. Instead let’s use independence. It’s clear that the result of one die is independent of the result of the other.
    🔗
    For the first question, to apply independence we first need to calculate how likely it is that one \(\drm20\) has a result \(\ge 10\text{.}\) This is a quick count: there are \(11\) numbers which are \(\ge 10\) and \(\le 20\text{,}\) so it is \(\frac{11}{20}\text{.}\) Then,
    \begin{equation*} P(\text{both at least } 10) = \left(\frac{11}{20}\right)^2 = \frac{121}{400}. \end{equation*}
    For the second question, it’s easiest to also use complements. Letting \(E\) denote the event “rolling at least one \(\ge 10\)” it’s easier to directly calculate the probability of the event \(\bar E = \) “both rolling \(\le 9\)”. Again, independence makes this easy. There are \(9\) results which are \(\le 9\text{.}\) Thus
    \begin{equation*} P(\bar E) = \frac9{20} \cdot \frac{9}{20} = \frac{81}{400}. \end{equation*}
    Finally, \(P(E) = 1 - \frac{81}{400} = \frac{319}{400}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 12.2.13.

    You draw a card from a deck, replace it and reshuffle, then draw again. How likely is it that you draw two spades?
    🔗
    🔗
    Be careful when you apply independence. If your events aren’t actually independent you can’t use the product equation. It’ll give you wrong answers.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 12.2.14.

    You draw a card from a deck, then draw another card without replacing the original. How likely is it that you draw the jack of spades both times?
    🔗
    Solution.
    If you naively try to use independence, you might calculate like this. For the first draw, there’s \(1\) outcome in \(52\) you are looking for, so the probability is \(\frac1{52}\text{.}\) For the second draw it’s \(\frac1{51}\text{,}\) because there’s one fewer draw. Therefore, the probability is
    \begin{equation*} \frac1{52} \cdot \frac1{51} = \frac1{2652}. \end{equation*}
    A small chance, but still a chance!.
    🔗
    If you think for a moment longer, however, you see that actually the probability is \(0\text{.}\) You can’t draw the same card twice, since you didn’t replace after the first draw! That is, the two draws weren’t actually independent.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The product equation is so helpful we’d like it to work even when things aren’t quite independent. Fortunately, there’s a way to make it happen.
    🔗
    Sometimes you can think of an event as a two step process—first \(X\) happens then \(Y\) happens. If you want to know how likely is it that \(X\) and \(Y\) both happen you can follow this two step process. First calculate how likely is it that \(X\) happens. Then, under the assumption that \(X\) has already happened, calculate how likely is it that \(Y\) also happens.
    🔗

    Example 12.2.15.

    Let’s use the same example of drawing the jack of spades twice. Think of it as a two step process. The first step is, drawing the jack of spades from \(52\) cards. This has probability \(\frac1{52}\text{.}\) For the second step, how likely is it to draw the jack of spades from the remaining cards? The answer is \(\frac0{51}\text{,}\) because none of the remaining cards is the jack of spades. So the overall probability is
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{52} \cdot \frac{0}{51} = 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 12.2.16.

    You draw two cards from a deck, without replacement. How likely is it they are both face cards?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Think of it as a two step process. For the first step, \(13\) of \(52\) cards are face cards, so the probability is \(\frac{12}{52} = \frac{3}{13}\text{.}\) For the second step, if you already drew one face card then \(11\) of the remaining \(51\) are face cards. So the probability you draw two face cards is
    \begin{equation*} \frac3{13} \cdot \frac{11}{51} = \frac{33}{663} = \frac{11}{221}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 12.2.17.

    You draw three cards from a deck, without replacement. How likely is it they are all black cards (that is, either spades or clubs)?
    🔗
    Hint.
    You know what to do for a two step process. How do you generalize that to three steps?
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 12.2.18.

    Explain how to calculate the probability of a four step process. Can you also do five steps? Six steps? What is the most general thing you can say?
    🔗
    🔗
    Here’s an explanation for why this way of thinking about two step processes gives you correct calculations. Imagine the sample space as a big rectangle. First you pick the row (the first step), then you pick the column (the second step), to pick a spot in the rectangle. (Note that this is exactly the cartesian product picture from Chapter 1.) We are only interested in spots in the rectangle with the correct first step. Inside these rows we only care those with the correct second step. Those might differ from row to row. For example, the row for “the first draw was \(J\spadesuit\)” will have \(K\heartsuit\) as a valid second choice, whereas the row for “the first draw was \(K\heartsuit\)” will not. But so long as there’s the same number in each row, we can rearrange the rows to form a rectangle. And the area of a rectangle is found by multiplying.
