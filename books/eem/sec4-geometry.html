    Section 4.3 The geometric meaning of integers

    In the last section we talked about how we build the integers from the natural numbers, namely throwing in additive inverses. Now we talk about a geometric interpretation of \(\Zbb\text{,}\) which will give us a way to understand the meaning of addition and multiplication.
    🔗
    Think of \(\Zbb\) as a line of dots, stretching infinitely far in both directions. One dot represents \(0\text{.}\) Moving right from it you count up in the positive numbers \(1, 2, 3, \ldots\) and moving left from it you count down in the negative numbers \(-1, -2, -3, \ldots\text{.}\) Call this picture the integer line.
    🔗
    In this picture, absolute value represents the number of steps to get to a number starting from \(0\text{.}\) For example, \(7\) and \(-7\) have the same absolute value—namely \(7\)—because they are the same distance from \(0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    
    The integer number line. Numbers, represented here as dots, march out infinitely in both directions. Infinitely many dots is a lot to draw, so arrows are placed on the line to encourage the viewer to imagine this finite image continuing on forever.
    🔗
    What this picture tells us is that positive versus negative is about direction. With the natural numbers, you could get bigger and bigger forever, but if you tried going the other way you’d eventually get stuck at \(0\text{.}\) With the integers, we add in an opposite direction which you can also travel infinitely.
    🔗
    This direction also tells us how to define order \(\lt\) for the integers.
    🔗

    Definition 4.3.1. Order \(\lt\) on integers.

    For two integers \(x\) and \(y\text{,}\) we say \(x \lt y\) if \(x\) is to the left of \(y\) on the number line. Put explicitly:
    • Any negative number is less than any positive number.
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(x\) and \(y\) are both \(\ge 0\) then \(x \lt y\) if and only if \(\abs x \lt \abs y\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • If \(x\) and \(y\) are both \(\le 0\) then \(x \lt y\) if and only if \(\abs x \gt \abs y\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    As we did with natural numbers, we use \(\le\text{,}\) \(\gt\) and \(\ge\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Aside
    The use of left for the negative direction and right for the positive direction is merely a convention. If your native language is, like English, written left-to-right this probably makes sense. Going forward means going to the right, going backward means going to the left. If your native language is written right-to-left, such as Arabic, then this might seem wrong, and you might ask if you could draw it differently. Everything would work just as well. What is important is that the negative direction and the positive direction are opposites.
    🔗
    This picture and the idea of positive versus negative as directions tells us the meaning of addition. Consider \(4 + 7 = 11\text{.}\) Think of this as meaning you start at \(4\) and you take \(7\) steps to the right. (Right because that is the positive direction.) Then you would end at \(11\text{.}\) Or consider \(4 - 7\text{.}\) This means starting at \(4\) and taking \(7\) steps to the left (the negative direction). Counting them off you see \(4 - 7 = -3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    In general, adding \(x\) means taking \(\abs x\) steps, where you go to th eright if \(x\) is positive and to the left if \(x\) is negative. (Adding \(0\) is taking no steps, which doesn’t change your position.)
    🔗
    You can think about in terms of a single starting and ending point, but it’s also useful to think about what addition does to the entire line. No matter your starting point, adding \(-3\) means taking \(3\) steps to the left. This is the entire line stepping \(3\) to the left.
    🔗
    
    Two integer number lines one placed above the other. Dashed arrows in between illustrate the movement of all numbers \(3\) steps to the left. This is what adding \(-3\) does to the number line.
    🔗
    Adding, for instance, \(2\) is the entire line stepping \(2\) to the right.
    🔗
    
    Two integer number lines one placed above the other. Dashed arrows in between illustrate the movement of all numbers \(2\) steps to the right. This is what adding \(2\) does to the number line.
    🔗
    Mathematicians liking to have too many names for things, we call these translations or shifts. In summary, the geometric meaning is translation. Adding by \(x\) shifts the entire number line \(\abs x\) units, with the sign of \(x\) telling which direction—left for negative, right for positive.
    🔗
    Now let’s think about multiplication. Consider \(2 \cdot 3\text{.}\) This takes the starting position at \(2\) and moves it \(3\) times as far away from \(0\text{,}\) namely to \(6\text{.}\) Or consider \(-1 \cdot 3\text{.}\) Like before, this moves the starting position \(3\) times as far away from \(0\text{,}\) ending at \(-3\text{.}\) Before this movement, our two starting points were only \(3\) steps apart, but at the end they were \(9\) steps apart. Multiplying stretched the number line so the points become farther apart.
    🔗
    In general, multiplying by a positive number stretches out the number line. If you multiply by the positive number \(x\) you stretch the line so points are \(x\) times farther apart. More formally, we call this a scaling.
    🔗
    
    Two integer number lines one placed above the other. Dashed arrows in between illustrate a scaling by a factor of \(2\text{,}\) corresponding to multiplication by \(2\text{.}\) All numbers are moved to be twice as far away from \(0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    How should we understand multiplication by a negative number? Let’s focus on the simplest case, namely multiplication by \(-1\text{.}\) Multiplying by \(-1\) should be the same as putting a minus sign in front of the number; more formally, multiplying \(x\) by \(-1\) should give you the additive inverse of \(x\text{.}\) What this amounts to is changing which side of the number line you are on. For example, \(3 \cdot -1 = -3\text{,}\) so it gets moved from the right side to the left, while \(-3 \cdot -1 = 3\text{,}\) so it gets moved from the left side to the right.
    🔗
    Altogether, what multiplication by \(-1\) does is flip the number line, swapping the left and right sides. More formally, we call this a reflection. You should think of this as swapping the two directions.
    🔗
    
    Two integer number lines one placed above the other. Dashed arrows in between illustrate a reflection, corresponding to multiplication by \(-1\text{.}\) All numbers are flipped to be on the other side of \(0\text{.}\) For legibility half of the dashed lines are not shown.
    🔗

    Aside
    Here’s an algebraic way to convince yourself that multiplying by \(-1\) should give the additive inverse. Consider the formula \(x \cdot (1 + (-1))\text{.}\) On the one hand, \(1 + (-1) = 0\) and so the product will be \(0\text{.}\) On the other hand, the distributivity property lets us rewrite this as \(x + (-1\cdot x)\text{.}\) Putting those together we get
    \begin{equation*} x + (-1 \cdot x) = 0\text{,} \end{equation*}
    whence we get that \(-1 \cdot x\) is the thing you add to \(x\) to get \(0\)—in other words, its additive inverse.
    🔗
    Consider now mulitplication by, say, \(-2\text{.}\) Think of this multiplication as a two-step process: \(-2 = -1\cdot 2\text{.}\) Geometrically, we reflect the number line and then scale by a factor of \(2\text{.}\) Or we could scale first and then reflect; the order doesn’t matter.
    🔗
    
    Three integer number lines one placed above each other in order. Dashed arrows in between the top and middle lines illustrate multiplication by \(2\text{,}\) a scaling so all points are twice as far from the center. Dashed arrows in between the middle and bottom lines illustrate a reflection, corresponding to multiplication by \(-1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Multiplication by \(0\) is a special case. Anything multiplied by \(0\) becomes \(0\text{,}\) so multiplying by \(0\) collapses the entire number line down to a point. This usually isn’t interesting or useful.
    🔗
    
    An illustration of the entire number being sent to the point at \(0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    To sum up this section, addition corresponds to translation, where the magnitude tells you how far to translate and the sign tells you what direction. Multiplication corresponds to scaling and reflection, where the magnitude tells you how much to scale by and the sign tells you whether to reflect.
