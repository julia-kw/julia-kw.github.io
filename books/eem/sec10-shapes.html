    Section 10.3 Other shapes

    The previous two sections focused on triangles. The concepts of congruence and similarity make sense for other shapes. Let’s start by reiterating the two definitions but not restricting them to triangles.
    🔗

    Definition 10.3.1. Congruence and similarity.

    Two shapes are congruent if one can be transformed into the other by a Euclidean transformation—a sequence of translations, rotations, and/or reflections. Two shapes are similar if one can be transformed into the other by a similarity—a sequence of uniform scalings, translations, rotations, and/or reflections.
    🔗
    🔗
    For some shapes, such as circles, these are trivial concepts because there’s not enough diversity for how the shapes can look.
    🔗

    Theorem 10.3.2.

    Any two circles are similar. Two circles are congruent if and only if they have the same radius.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    If you have two circles, a translation moves one so that their centers are the same point. If they have the same radius, they exactly overlap. If not, a uniform rescaling makes them exactly overlap.
    🔗
    🔗
    You can think of this fact like this. If you are okay with zooming in or out, then any two circles look the same. If you don’t want to allow zooming, then two circles look the same only when they have the same radius. That is, the radius of a circle determines everything about a circle and there is no information beyond the radius. Contrast to triangles, which require multiple data to specify.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 10.3.3.

    Explain why any two squares are similar. When are two squares congruent?
    🔗
    Hint.
    This is very similar to circles.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The same is true for regular polygons of any number of sides, as you will explain for homework.
    🔗
    For other shapes, these aren’t useful concepts because there’s too many possibilities for how the shapes can look. Nonetheless we will say what we can.
    🔗
    Let’s start with an observation. If two shapes are similar, then they have the same number of sides. Moreover, they have the same number of curved sides and same number of straight sides. This is because none of the transformations which compose a similarity—namely uniform scalings, translations, rotations, and reflections—can create a bend or corner. The converse is badly false. If you draw two random shapes with the same number of sides they almost certainly will not be similar.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 10.3.4.

    Draw two random pentagons. Explain why they aren’t similar.
    🔗
    🔗
    Another observation: If two shapes are similar then they have the same angles. That is, there is a way to label the angles \(A,B,\ldots\) and \(A',B',\ldots\) in order either clockwise or counterclockwise so that \(A = A'\text{,}\) \(B = B'\text{,}\) and so on. This is because the transformations which comprise a similarity cannot change angles.
    🔗
    With triangles, the converse was true. Triangles with the same angles are similar. This isn’t the case in general. For example, a square and a (non-square) rectangle have the same angles but aren’t similar.
    🔗
    Similarly, if two shapes are congruent then they have the same angles and the same sides. This is because Euclidean transformations cannot change angles nor distances. That is, there is a way to label the angles \(A,B,\ldots\) and \(A',B',\ldots\) and sides \(a,b,\ldots\) and \(a',b',\ldots\) in order either clockwise or counterclockwise so that \(A = A'\text{,}\) \(a = a'\text{,}\) \(B = B'\text{,}\) \(b = b'\text{,}\) and so on.
    🔗
    Similarity is a useful concept because knowing one shape is an enlarged version of another gives you information about its measurements.
    🔗

    Theorem 10.3.5.

    Suppose a shape A is a uniform scaling of shape B by a factor of \(K\text{.}\) Then any one dimensional measurement of A, for example height or perimeter, is \(K\) times the corresponding measurement of B. And any two dimensional measurement of A, for example area, is \(K^2\) times the corresponding measurement of \(B\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 10.3.6.

    A genie tells you that the area of a regular pentagon with side length \(1\) is
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\sqrt{25 + 10\sqrt{5}}}{4} \approx 1.72. \end{equation*}
    Without knowing how the genie came up with that complicated formula, figure out the area of a regular pentagon with side length \(3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Regular pentagons are all similar, so the one with side length \(3\) is a uniform scaling of the one with side length \(1\) by a factor of \(\frac{3}{1} = 3\text{.}\) Thus we simply need to multiply the area by \(3^2\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{9\sqrt{25 + 10\sqrt{5}}}{4} \approx 15.5. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    On this topic it’s also worth noting the affect of non-uniform scalings. Suppose you non-uniformly scale a shape by a factor of \(K\text{.}\) If a one dimensional measurement is entirely in the direction of the scaling then it is multiplied by \(K\text{.}\) If the measurement is entirely perpendicular to the direction of the scaling then it doesn’t change. If it’s in between, then it’s changed by an amount between \(1\) and \(K\text{,}\) but it’s not simple to determine the exact value. On the other hand, the area of the shape is scaled by \(K\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 10.3.7.

    You can think of any rectangle as coming from two non-uniform scalings of the unit square with side lengths \(1\text{.}\) Namely, a rectangle with width \(x\) and height \(y\) came from the unit square by doing a horizontal scaling by a factor of \(x\) and a vertical scaling by a factor of \(y\text{.}\) Note that the horizontal scaling doesn’t affect the length of the vertical side and the vertical scaling doesn’t affect the length of the horizontal side.
    🔗
    Knowing that the area of the unit square is \(1\text{,}\) you can get the formula for the area of this rectangle. Namely, the area is scaled by \(x\) and by \(y\text{,}\) for a total scaling of \(xy\text{.}\) Since the starting area was \(1\text{,}\) the area of the rectangle is \(xy\text{.}\)
