    Section 12.4 The meaning of probability: Bayesianism

    In the previous sections, we talked about probability as coming from counting. This fits nicely with the frequentist interpretation of probability from Section 12.1. Recall that this interpretation gave the meaning as \(P(E)\text{,}\) the probability of an event \(E\text{,}\) is the fraction of outcomes in \(E\) you expect to see with many identitcal repeated random trials. If all atomic outcomes in the sample space \(S\) are equally likely then you expect to see
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\card E}{\card S} = \frac{\text{number of events in } E}{\text{number of events in } S} \end{equation*}
    fraction of them in \(E\text{,}\) so that is the probability.
    🔗
    While this view of what probability can be used in many places, it struggles in some important applications. For example, in Chapter 11 we talked about statistical inference. This amounted to asking about probability: assuming the null hypothesis we want to reject, how likely is it to see outcomes this extreme?
    🔗
    It’s difficult to put this into the frequentist framework of repeated random trials. Many statistical experiments are not repeatable. For example, perhaps you are trying to forecast an election and determine the odds for who wins. You can’t run the election over and over to see how likely each outcome is. So a statement like “Senator Example has a \(74\%\) chance of winning reelection” can’t have the frequentist meaning.
    🔗
    In this section we will discuss a different interpretation of probability which better handles these cases. Along the way we will talk about conditional probability, which is the key concept in this interpretation.
    🔗
    We have seen conditional probability before, though not by that name. With statistical inference in Chapter 11 we asked questions like “assuming the null hypothesis, how likely is it to see an outcome this extreme?” In Section 12.2 when talking about multi-step processes we asked questions like “assuming the outcome of the first step is known, how likely is the second step to be \(Y\text{?}\)” Conditional probability is about these sorts of calculations; assuming you have partial information, how does that determine the likelihood of outcomes?
    🔗

    Definition 12.4.1. Conditional probability.

    Suppose \(E\) and \(F\) are events in a probability space with sample space \(S\text{.}\) The conditional probability of \(E\) assuming \(F\text{,}\) written \(P(E \mid F)\text{,}\) is the likelihood that \(E\) happens assuming you know that \(F\) happens. It can be calculated as
    \begin{equation*} P(E \mid F) = \frac{P(E \cap F)}{P(F)}. \end{equation*}
    In case you are dealing with uniform probability, it can equivalently be calculated by counting:
    \begin{equation*} P(E \mid F) = \frac{\card{E \cap F}}{\card F}. \end{equation*}
    Like many important concepts, this one goes by many names. You see people talk about the probabability of \(E\) given \(F\) or the the probability of \(E\) conditional on \(F\) or other ways to express conditional probability.
    🔗
    🔗
    To explain the formulas. If you assume \(F\) happens, there are \(\card F\) many possible outcomes. How many of those are in \(E\text{?}\) It would overcount to say \(\card E\text{,}\) since some of \(E\) may be outside of \(F\text{.}\) The correct count is \(\card {E \cap F}\text{,}\) the number of outcomes in both \(E\) and \(F\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Note that conditional probability only makes sense if \(P(F) > 0\text{.}\) If \(F\) never happens then it doesn’t make sense to ask how likely something is assuming \(F\) happens.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 12.4.2.

    You roll \(3\drm 6\text{.}\) Assuming the first die rolls a \(4\text{,}\) how likely is it that the results sum to at least \(14\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    This is a conditional probability. We could look at all \(6^3 = 216\) outcomes of rolling three dice, but it’s easier to think only about the outcomes where the first die is \(4\text{.}\) There are \(6^2 = 36\) outcomes to look at, since the remaining two dice rolls independently have \(6\) outcomes each. We need to count how many of these outcomes sum to at least \(10\text{.}\) We can do list them:
    \begin{align*} 6 + 4 \amp\quad 6 + 5 \amp\quad 6 + 6\\ \amp\quad 5 + 5 \amp\quad 5 + 6\\ \amp \amp\quad 6 + 6 \end{align*}
    These are \(3 + 2 + 1 = 6\) outcomes, so the conditional probability is
    \begin{equation*} \frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 12.4.3.

    Suppose \(E\) and \(F\) are events in a probability space. Then we can calculate their joint probability \(P(E \cap F)\) as
    \begin{equation*} P(E \cap F) = P(E \mid F) \cdot P(F). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    This falls out from writing these various probabilities as fractions. First let’s write them as counts for uniform probability, where \(S\) is the sample space:
    \begin{align*} P(E \cap F) \amp \amp P(E \mid F) \cdot P(F)\\ \frac{\card{E \cap F}}{\card S} \amp \amp \frac{\card{E \cap F}}{\card F} \cdot \frac{\card F}{\card S}\\ \frac{\card{E \cap F}}{\card S} \amp = \amp \frac{\card{E \cap F}}{\card S}. \end{align*}
    Alternatively you can write solely in terms of probabilities:
    \begin{align*} P(E \cap F) \amp \amp P(E \mid F) \cdot P(F)\\ P(E \cap F) \amp \amp \frac{P(E \cap F)}{P(F)} \cdot P(F)\\ P(E \cap F) \amp = \amp P(E \cap F) \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 12.4.4.

    You roll \(3\drm 6\text{.}\) How likely is it that the first die rolls a \(4\) and they sum to at least \(14\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    Use conditional probability! You want to calculate \(P(E \cap F)\text{,}\) where \(E = \) “the first die is \(6\)” and \(F = \) “the sum is at least \(14\)”. In an earlier checkpoint we already calculated \(P(F \mid E)\) so what remains is to calculate \(P(E)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    You might wonder if there’s any relationship between the two directions of conditional probability—\(P(E \mid F)\) versus \(P(F \mid E)\text{.}\) An important probability fact tells us they are related.
    🔗

    Theorem 12.4.5. Bayes’s theorem.

    Consider events \(E\) and \(F\) in a sample space, where both events have nonzero probability. Then
    \begin{equation*} P(E \mid F) = \frac{P(F \mid E) P(E)}{P(F)}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    This is surprisingly straightforward. The key idea is to unpack the definition of conditional probability: \(P(F \mid E) = P(F \cap E) / P(E)\text{.}\) Substituting this into the righthand side of the equation we get
    \begin{equation*} \frac{P(F \mid E) P(E)}{P(F)} = \frac{\frac{P(F \cap E)}{P(E)} \cdot P(E)}{P(F)} = \frac{P(F \cap E)}{P(F)}. \end{equation*}
    Now note that \(P(F \cap E)/P(F)\) is by definition of conditional probabilty, the same as \(P(E \mid F)\text{.}\) But that’s the lefthand side of the equation.
    🔗
    🔗
    A classic example of the use of Bayes’s theorem is determining the reliability of a test. Suppose you are tested for a rare disease, and it comes up positive. How likely is it you actually have the disease? You might say it’s very likely, since the test usually gets the correct answer. That is, false positives—the test wrongly coming up positive—and false negatives—the test wrongly coming up negative—are rare.
    🔗
    Let’s do an example calculation to test this gut reaction.
    🔗

    Example 12.4.6.

    Disease \(X\) has a \(0.1\%\) incidence rate, meaning it occurs in \(\frac1{1000}\) of the population. A test for disease \(X\) has a \(99\%\) chance of giving the correct diagnosis, whether positive or negative. You test positive. How likely is it you have disease \(X\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Let’s give some names to these events. Call \(X\) the event “you have the disease” and \(T\) the event “the test came back positive”. What we are trying to calculate is the conditional probability \(P(X \mid T)\text{.}\) We know a few probabilities:
    \begin{align*} P(X) \amp= 0.001,\\ P(T \mid X) \amp= 0.99,\\ P(T \mid \bar X) \amp= 0.01. \end{align*}
    The first is the incidence rate, the second is the true positive rate, and the last is the false positive rate. Because we know the conditional probability in the opposite direction, you might think we could use Bayes’s theorem. You are correct. We will calculate
    \begin{equation*} P(X \mid T) = \frac{P(T \mid X) \cdot P(X)}{P(T)}. \end{equation*}
    You can think of the fraction on the right as follows. The numerator is what fraction of the population both has disease \(X\) and has a positive test, while the denominator is how many have a positive test. We have to do a small amount of work to calculate the denominator, since we aren’t given it directly. To calculate what fraction of the population have a positive test, we add together the true positives with the false positives. The true positives are given by the formula
    \begin{equation*} P(T \mid X) \cdot P(X) = 0.99 \cdot 0.001 = 0.00099. \end{equation*}
    Similarly, the false negatives are given by the formula
    \begin{equation*} P(T \mid \bar X) \cdot P(\bar X) = 0.01 \cdot 0.999 = 0.00999. \end{equation*}
    (We know \(P(\bar X)\) because that’s how many don’t have the disease, namely \(1 - P(X)\text{.}\)) Adding those together we get
    \begin{align*} P(T) \amp= P(T \mid X) \cdot P(X) + P(T \mid \bar X) \cdot P(\bar X)\\ \amp= 0.00099 + 0.0099 = .01098. \end{align*}
    Now we plug the three values into Bayes’s theorem:
    \begin{align*} P(X \mid T) \amp= \frac{P(T \mid X) \cdot P(X)}{P(T)}\\ \amp= \frac{0.00099}{0.01098} \approx 0.09 = 9\%. \end{align*}
    That is, there’s only about a \(9\%\) chance you have the disease.
    🔗
    🔗
    Maybe you are surprised by this calculation. If the test is \(99\%\) accurate, why is it overwhelming likely it gave the wrong answer? The reason is, the accuracy of the test was less than the incidence rate of the disease. As such, the false positives outnumbered the true positives. The lesson here is, the accuracy of a test needs to match or succeed the incidence rate to give good info.
    🔗

    Aside
    Some details are being swept under the rug for simplicity. Let me highlight one. In practice, the incidence rate across the entire population isn’t what you want to look at. Rather, you want to look at a subpopulation, such as only women over the age of \(55\) or only people exhibiting XYZ symptom. The reason is, if it’s a rare disease the test isn’t being given to everyone. Instead, it’s only given to people particularly at risk or for whom there’s already reason to suspect they might have it.
    🔗
    So the level of accuracy you need for the test is lower than what you’d think you need if you looked at the incidence rate across the entire population. In this example, maybe the disease only occurs in \(0.1\%\) of the population but is in \(10\%\) of the relevant subpopulation. If you rerun the calculation with \(P(X) = 0.1\) you’ll get a much higher chance that you actally have the disease.
    🔗
    This example illustrates one practical use of Bayes’s theorem. The main purpose in mentioning it, however, is to set up the discussion for an interpretation of probability different from the frequentist’s.
    🔗

    Definition 12.4.7. The Bayesian interpretation of probability.

    The Bayesian interpretation of probability, also called the subjective interpretation, gives the following meaning. A probability \(P(E)\) represents a degree of confidence that the event \(E\) will happen. For example, if \(E\) is the event of a coin flip coming up heads you might assign \(P(E) = \frac12\text{.}\) However, if you happen to know that the coin is biased to come up tails most of the time you’d assign it a much lower chance.
    🔗
    For a more interesting example, suppose \(E\) is the event that your preferred candidate wins the upcoming election. This is something it’s hard to make sense of with frequentism, but here \(P(E)\) is your degree of confidence in this outcome. You could be more or less rational about this. Maybe it’s based entirely on your conviction that your candidate is the best one so everyone should agree, or maybe it’s based on rigorous polling and election simulation. Either way, the number \(P(E)\) is a measure of your subjective confidence.
    🔗
    🔗
    A feature of this interpretation is that, as one of the names for it suggests, probability is subjective. Rather than being an objective fact, the degree of confidence can vary from person to person. Some might find this uncomfortable—isn’t mathematics about uncovering objective truths about reality? Perhaps you find this reason to reject this view of what probability is.
    🔗
    If you do you are not alone. The interpretation of what probability means is a difficult topic that has been the subject of much scholarly debate, with many arguments slung for many different conclusions. A good introduction to this topic is the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy article on interpretations of philosophy. But this is too big a topic and too big a detour for us to go into.
    🔗
    A distinctive feature of Bayesianism is its use of Bayes’s theorem to give a rule for updating your probabilities (that is, degrees of confidence). Take coin flipping as an example. Without any further information, your prior probability that a coin comes up heads is probably \(\frac12\text{.}\) But if you flip in multiple times and it repeatedly comes up heads, you’ll suspect the coin is weighted and want to update your confidence to have a different posterior probability.
    🔗
    The calculations get involved, so let’s skip over them to focus on the big picture. The key point is, Bayes’s theorem provides a process you can use to update beliefs based on new evidence. This process, called Bayesian inference, has many applications. One of them is spam filtering for email. The problem is, you want to determine whether an email is spam, but you can’t know with \(100\%\) certainty. Instead, the idea is that certain features adjust the likelihood that an email is spam—coming from a contact lowers the likelihood, the existence of common spam phrases increases it. You train the filter, going through repeated cycles of updating probabilities. At the end you have a program that identifies spam with a high degree of accuracy.
    🔗
    There are many other applications of Bayesian inference. This is a an instance where you can point to a topic in elementary mathematics (probability) and tell students how it gets used in technology they see every day.
