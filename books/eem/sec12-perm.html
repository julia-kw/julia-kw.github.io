    Section 12.3 Permutations and combinations

    A key idea we ended on in the last section is that if an event can be thought of as a multistep process, you can determine its event by multiplying. In this section we build on that idea by looking at some common counting problems.
    🔗
    We begin with a well-known example, known as the birthday problem.
    🔗

    Example 12.3.1.

    You are at a party with \(20\) people total. Is it more likely that two people share a birthday or that everyone has different birthdays?
    🔗
    To answer this, we will make a couple simplifying assumptions. First, we will exclude leap day birthdays, which occur approximately every four years. Second, we will assume that all \(365\) birthdays are equally likely. These assumptions will mean our probability calculation won’t be \(100\%\) correct. But it will be close enough to know which outcome is more likely.
    🔗
    We are dealing with complementary events, so knowing one’s probability lets us easily compute the other. As such we should ask, which of the two is easier to calculate? Take a moment to ask that.
    🔗
    Perhaps you answered that it’s easier to calculate how likely it is that everyone has a different birthday. I agree. We can think of this as a twenty step process—pick the first birthday, then the second one, and so on, each time having to pick a different one. On the other hand, it’s hard to model two people sharing a birthday as a multi-step process—which two?
    🔗
    For the first step, there are \(365\) birthdays and \(365\) are free to claim without conflict. For the second person, there are \(365\) birthdays and \(364\) are free to claim without conflict. After all, there’s only one day that’s off-limit so far. Each step we get one fewer allowed birthday while still having \(365\) to choose from. Thus we get the following big product to calculate:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{365}{365} \cdot \frac{364}{365} \cdot \frac{363}{365} \cdot \cdots \cdot \frac{346}{365}. \end{equation*}
    At this point we pull out a computer because this is tedious to do by hand. The probability is
    \begin{equation*} {\tiny \frac{1,036,690,753,342,460,944,844,102,991,873,410,166,195,245,875,200,000} {1,761,397,149,289,626,339,965,899,029,490,128,250,217,437,744,140,625}} \approx 58.9\%. \end{equation*}
    That is it is only slightly more likely that all birthdays are different than that two people share a birthday. This is even though there’s only twenty people at the party, much less than the \(365\) possible birthdays.
    🔗
    If there’s instead \(30\) people at the party, you can run a similar calculation. In this case, the probability everyone has a different birthday is \(\approx 29.4\%\text{.}\) That is, with only \(30\) people it’s much more likely to have two people share a birthday than it is otherwise. It’s at \(23\) people that this tipping point occurs and it becomes more likely to have a shared birthday.
    🔗
    Some people call this the birthday paradox, because they think it doesn’t make sense for a shared birthday to be more common than not when you have so much fewer people than days in the year. But our calculations show this expectation is flawed.
    🔗
    🔗
    Let’s introduce some notation for this kind of counting problem.
    🔗

    Definition 12.3.2. Factorials and permutations.

    A permutation of a finite list of things is a way to order those things. The factorial function, written \(n!\text{,}\) counts how many permutations there are for a list of \(n\) many things.
    🔗
    We can calculate factorials using the rule that \(n!\) is the product of all whole numbers from \(n\) down to \(1\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} n! = n(n-1)(n-2) \cdots 1. \end{equation*}
    Alternatively, we can use these rules
    \begin{align*} n! \amp = n \cdot (n-1)!\\ 0! \amp = 1. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    To understand the formula, think of picking a permutation as an \(n\) step process. First you pick the first object, then the second, then the third, and so on until there are none left. You start with \(n\) choices, and each step has one fewer. For example, if you want to order \(5\) things there are
    \begin{equation*} 5! = 5 \cdot 4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 = 120 \end{equation*}
    ways to do it.
    🔗
    That \(0! = 1\) is the sort of thing that mathematicians think is natural and obvious but makes normal people upset. Let me defend the choice. How many ways are there to order zero things? The answer, of course, is one. There is only one way to do it, namely to do nothing. Think, how many ways are there to order zero books on a shelf: just one, namely leaving the shelf bare.
    🔗
    If you don’t find that satsifactory, here are two more arguments. First, \(0!\) is the product of zero many numbers, and if you multiple zero numbers together you should get the multiplicative identity \(1\text{.}\) This is the same reasoning as why \(a^0 = 1 \) for any base \(a\text{.}\) Second, there are useful formulas involving factorials that make sense when to talk about with \(0!\text{.}\) For these formulas, having \(0! = 1\) gives the correct answer. (We will see one of these formulas soon when we talk about combinatins.) So this is a practical choice.
    🔗

    Aside
    Not so secretly this practical choice is the original answer in a beaglepuss. Why do these formulas give the correct answer when we say \(0! = 1\text{?}\) Because what we are counting is the number of ways to order zero things.
    🔗
    With the birthday problem example, we didn’t calculate \(365!\) but rather something close to it. We weren’t asking how many ways to order \(365\) birthdays but rather only picked twenty of them. Let’s also give a name to this concept.
    🔗

    Definition 12.3.3. Falling factorial.

    Consider nonnegative numbers \(n\) and \(k\) with \(k \le n\text{.}\) Define the falling factorial \((n)_k\) as the product of \(k\) many integers in sequence counting down from \(n\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} (n)_k = n \cdot (n-1) \cdot \cdots (n-k+1). \end{equation*}
    Note that to have \(k\) many numbers multiplied, the last one should be \(n - (k-1)\text{,}\) not \(n-k\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The falling factorial \((n)_k\) counts the number of ways to order \(k\) many things from \(n\) options. Note that \((n)_n = n!\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Note that
    \begin{equation*} (n)_k = \frac{n!}{(n-k)!}. \end{equation*}
    But the latter formula isn’t helpful in practice since it requires you to divide two large numbers. For example, it’s easier to compute \((10)_2 = 10 \cdot 9 = 90\) rather than compute
    \begin{equation*} \frac{10!}{8!} = \frac{3628800}{40320}. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Checkpoint 12.3.4.

    Explain the equality in the above paragraph. Why is
    \begin{equation*} (n)_k = \frac{n!}{(n-k)!}? \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 12.3.5.

    There are eight swimmers in a race. How many different possible outcomes are there for who takes the top three positions?
    🔗
    To count this, we can think of it as a three step process. There’s \(8\) many possibliities for who gets first. Once that’s chosen, there’s \(7\) many possibilities for second, and then \(6\) many possibilities for third. So in all there are
    \begin{equation*} (8)_3 = 8 \cdot 7 \cdot 6 = 336 \end{equation*}
    possible outcomes for the top three.
    🔗
    Note that if you want to know about the order of all eight swimmers there’s many more possible outcomes, namely \(8! = 40320\) many. If we only care about the top three then, for instance, we aren’t going to count it as a different outcome if seventh and eighth swap places. As such there are much fewer outcomes to count.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 12.3.6.

    There are twenty people at a party. How many different ways are there for them to all have different birthdays? (Discount leap days.)
    🔗
    We’ve seen this counting problem before. There’s \(365\) options for the first person, then as we go along there’s one fewer option for each person. Thus in total there are
    \begin{equation*} (365)_{20} = 365 \cdot 364 \cdot \cdots \cdot 346 \end{equation*}
    many possibilities.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 12.3.7.

    You choose a PIN at random for your ATM card. If the PIN is four digits, how likely is it that every digit differs?
    🔗
    Solution.
    This is a question about uniform probability, so it reduces to two counting problems. We want to calculate
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\text{number of PINs with no repeat}}{\text{number of PINs}}. \end{equation*}
    Let’s count the denominator first. Choosing a PIN is a four step process, with each step having \(10\) options: the digits \(0\) through \(9\text{.}\) So in total there are
    \begin{equation*} 10 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 = 10^4 \end{equation*}
    many possible PINs.
    🔗
    The numerator is also chosen by a four step process, except this time each step you have one fewer option than before. Thus the numerator is counted by
    \begin{equation*} (10)_4 = 10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8 \cdot 7. \end{equation*}
    In all, the probability a random PIN doesn’t repeat a digit is
    \begin{equation*} \frac{(10)_4}{10^4} = \frac{5040}{10000} = \frac{63}{125} = 50.4\%. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Note that this was essentially the same as the birthday problem. The numbers were different—\(4\) choices from \(10\) options rather than \(20\) choices from \(365\) options. But it’s the same general formula of falling factorial over an exponent:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{(n)_k}{n^k}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Factorials and falling factorials count selection processes where the order matters—you care about what comes first versus second. Many selection processes are not like that. For example, maybe you need to select three students from a class of twenty to do a group project. It doesn’t make sense to say who is picked first versus second versus third. If you tried to count this as \((20)_3 = 6840\) you would be way overcounting, since that would count picking Alice then Bob then Carol and different from picking Bob then Alice then Carol.
    🔗
    We need a different device to count this.
    🔗

    Definition 12.3.8. Binomial coefficients.

    Consider nonnegative numbers \(n\) and \(k\) with \(k \le n\text{.}\) Define the binomial coefficient, also called a combination, \(n \choose k\) as
    \begin{equation*} {n \choose k} = \frac{(n)_k}{k!} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}. \end{equation*}
    The binomial coefficient \(n \choose k\) counts the number of ways to choose \(k\) many things from \(n\) options, where you don’t care about order.
    🔗
    🔗
    The binomial coefficient is so named for its connection to coefficients of the binomial power \((x + y)^n\text{,}\) but explaining this connection would be too far a detour for us.
    🔗

    Example 12.3.9.

    You are picking three students from a class of twenty to do a group project. How many ways are there to do this?
    🔗
    We use the binomial coefficient to count this:
    \begin{equation*} {20 \choose 3} = \frac{(20)_3}{3!} = \frac{20 \cdot 19 \cdot 18}{3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1} = 1140. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Let’s see where this formula comes from. Here’s a way to think about this counting problem. To pick three students from twenty, pick a way to order all twenty students, then pick the first three in the order. There’s \(20!\) ways to do this. However, there’s not \(20!\) different outcomes. If you swap the first and second student you get the same group of three. Or if you swap the thirteenth and nineteenth. So we need to divide out by the amount we are overcounting. One way we overcount is that rearranging the first three students doesn’t change the outcome. There’s \(3!\) ways to order them, so we need to divide by \(3!\text{.}\) The other way we overcount is the rearring the last seventeen students doesn’t change the outcome. So we also need to divide by \(17!\text{,}\) the number of ways to do that. In all, our count is
    \begin{equation*} \frac{20!}{3!17!} = {20 \choose 3}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    In general, if you are choosing \(k\) things from \(n\) where you don’t care about order, you can think of the process as ordering all \(n\) things and keeping the first \(k\text{.}\) There’s \(n!\) ways to do this, but we’re overcounting by factors of \(k!\) (you can reorder the first \(k\)) and \((n-k)!\) (you can reorder the last \(n-k\)). In all the count is
    \begin{equation*} \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!} = {n \choose k}. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Checkpoint 12.3.10.

    You are choosing three students from a class of twenty to do a group project. How likely is it that Alice is in the group?
    🔗
    Solution.
    We need to calculate
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\text{number of groups with Alice}}{\text{number of groups}}. \end{equation*}
    We’ve already counted that the denominator is \({20 \choose 3} = 1140\text{.}\) It remains to count the numerator. For this, we ask how ways are there to pick a group that includes Alice? For this, Alice’s place is already fixed so we just need to pick the remaining two students. There’s ninteen options for them (you already picked Alice). So the numerator is \({19 \choose 2} = 171 \text{.}\) Thus the likelihood you put Alice in the group is
    \begin{equation*} \frac{{19 \choose 2}}{{20 \choose 3}} = \frac{171}{1140} = \frac{3}{20} = 15\%. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 12.3.11.

    Your department of \(10\) people is picking \(5\) people to form a committee. This committee will have a chairperson. If all choices are made randomly, how likely is it that you end up on the committee? How likely is it that you are the chairperson?
    🔗
    Hint.
    The first question is similar to the previous exercise. For the second, think of it as a two step process: first pick the committee, then pick the chair. So to calculate the probabliity that you are on the committee and are the chair you can multiply the probablity that you are on the committee by the probability that, assuming you’re on the committee, you are chosen as chair.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 12.3.12.

    You randomly divide a class of ten students into two equally sized groups. In this class are Alice and Bob, who hate each other. How likely is it that Alice and Bob end up in the same group?
    🔗
    Hint.
    You can’t count this directly using binomial coefficients. Instead, you need to think similar to how we got the formula for \(n \choose k\text{.}\) Think of your choice as being made by ordering the ten students and splitting the first five from the last five. How likely is it that Alice and Bob are on the same side? Is it easier to do the count if you assume you put Alice first and then ordered the remaining students?
