    Section 8.1 Length and perimeter

    Length is the measure of the size or quantity of a one-dimensional shape such as a line or curve. To give a number you must say what the unit of the measurement is. When you measure a distance, are you measuring in meters, feet, miles, what? Depending on your choice of unit you will get different answers. When working abstractly, we not talk about units and assume we know what a distance of \(1\) means. For example, when talking about a rectangular track of land it might measure \(12\) meters by \(20\) meters. But when talking about a rectangle as an abstract shape we would say it’s \(12\) by \(20\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For lines you can imagine measuring length as having a yardstick. You put the yardstick up against the line from end to end and the measuring stick tells you the length. For curves that won’t work, since your measuring device is a line. You could use a flexible measuring tape. Alternatively, you can approximate the measure by measuring lots of short, approximately linear pieces and adding them all up. As the pieces get shorter and shorter—and you get more and more pieces to measure—you will get a more and more accurate measure. If the pieces are infinitely small then the error in your measurement will also be infinitely small.
    🔗
    This process is not practical. No one wants to measure the length of, say, a circle by using trillions of tiny rulers and adding up the little lengths. Fortunately there are formulas we can use to determine the length around the outside of a shape.
    🔗

    Definition 8.1.1. Perimeter.

    The perimeter of a shape is the length of its boundary. For circles we also call the perimeter the circumference.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 8.1.2. Perimeter of a polygon.

    The perimeter of a polygon is the sum of its side lengths. Here are a few special cases of this.
    • If a square has side length \(s\) its perimeter is \(4s\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • If a rectangle has length \(\ell\) and width \(w\) its perimeter is \(2\ell + 2w\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • If a regular \(n\)-gon has side length \(s\) its perimeter is \(ns\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    The perimeters are found by adding side lengths together.
    🔗
    🔗
    You can memorize the formulas for the special cases, but they all come from the same idea: you’re adding up side lengths. So if you ever forget a formula you can rediscover by remembering that key idea.
    🔗
    For shapes with curves the perimeter isn’t based on this one idea and you need a different way to get at the formula.
    🔗

    Definition 8.1.3. Radius and diameter.

    The radius of a circle is the distance from its center to its boundary. The diameter is the distance from a point on the boundary to the opposite point, equal to twice the radius.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 8.1.4. Circumference of a circle.

    If the radius of a circle is \(r\) then its circumference is
    \begin{equation*} 2\pi r. \end{equation*}
    Equivalently, if the diameter is \(d\) then the circumference is \(\pi d\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    There is some number \(x\) so that the circumference is \(x\) times the radius. We define \(\pi\) so that that number is \(2\pi\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    This “proof” is not enlightening. While a mathematician might accept “it’s true by definition” as an explanation a normal person will not. To satisfy the normal person then let’s talk a little.
    🔗
    First off it’d be nice to have a value for \(\pi\) we can write down. The best we can do is give an approximation. The number \(\pi\) turns out to be an irrational number, so it cannot be written exactly as a fraction of whole numbers and its decimal expansion never repeats. Here’s the first many digits of \(\pi\text{;}\) for practical purposes it’s almost always enough to stop at two digits past the decimal point.
    \begin{equation*} \pi \approx 3.1415926535897932384626433 8327950288419716939937510\ldots \end{equation*}
    One way you can approximate \(\pi\text{,}\) first used by the ancient Greek mathematician Archimedes, is by fitting a circle of radius \(1\) between regular polygons.
    🔗
    
    A circle with an inscribed square and a circumscribed square. inscribed square, the largest possible square entirely contained inside the circle, touches the circumference of the circle at its vertices. The circumscribed square, the smallest square which entirely contains the circle, touches the circumference of the circle at the midpoints of its sides. The area of the circle sits between the more easily calculated areas of the circles.
    🔗
    The perimeters of these polygons can be calculated with some effort. This gives both an upper and lower bound for the circumference \(= 2\pi\text{,}\) whence you get bounds for the value of \(\pi\text{.}\) Early precursors to calculus were used by Indian mathematicians in the 16th century to produce yet better approximations. These methods were improved upon with later European work in calculus to determine yet more digits of \(\pi\text{.}\) With digital computers coming into the scene in the 20th century tedious human calculations could now be done by machine. Modern work has determined the digits of \(\pi\) out to billions of places.
    🔗
    This quest for more digits is, one must admit, quixotic. For accurate real world calculations only a few digits are needed.
    🔗
    For other curved shapes, methods similar to those used for the circle can be applied to approximate perimeters. Throwing in tools from calculus you can sometimes work out exact formulas. Suffice it to say, in general determining perimeters can be difficult.
    🔗
    But if things stay straight then we can know a lot with just one formula. This formula is so important it deserves its own section.
    🔗
